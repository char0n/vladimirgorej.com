<p class="lead">
  <i>Technical Content Strategy Decoded</i> by Adam Duvander is the follow-up to <a href="{% link _book-reviews/2026-07-12-developer-marketing-does-not-exist.md %}">Developer Marketing Does Not Exist</a>, and it narrows the focus from developer marketing in general to one thing: the content you publish and why it does or does not attract the right technical audience. It is a short book. I read it in one evening, and by the end I had a clear picture of its main concepts, which is exactly what a book like this should deliver.
</p>

The starting point will be familiar to anyone who read the first book: **be a teacher, not a marketer**. Your job is to educate your audience about the problem space, not to sell them the product. Duvander builds on that with a second role I found even more useful: the **translator**. You sit between your audience and your product, and your content has to connect the two. That means understanding who your audience actually is, learning the common language they use to describe their problems, and speaking that language back to them instead of your internal product vocabulary. The book also catalogs the common mistakes people make in this translator role, which is a useful checklist to hold your own content against.

The core of the book is what Duvander calls the **technical engagement system**, three dials summarized as **P.A.T.: Problem, Angle, Teach**. Every piece of content starts from a problem your audience is having, takes a specific angle on it, and then teaches the solution, with your product appearing only as part of what is being taught. This ordering is the whole point. People search for solutions to their problems, not for your product, so content that leads with the product is invisible to the people you most want to reach.

From there the book gets practical about **establishing a concept catalog**: a curated set of the concepts your audience needs to understand, which becomes the backbone of your content plan. Instead of chasing whatever topic seems interesting this week, you build authority around a defined set of concepts and let individual articles hang off them. I liked this because it turns content strategy from an ad hoc activity into something you can actually plan and maintain.

Duvander also spends time on **what matters and what does not**. Excessive SEO tactics, vanity metrics, and volume for its own sake fall into the second category. The numbers most people track are, in his view, the wrong ones, and the book is direct about which ones actually matter.

One thing I missed is the breakdown of content types that made the first book so practical. <i>Developer Marketing Does Not Exist</i> separated blog posts, tutorials, and guides, described what each one is for, and even prescribed a structure for a good tutorial. This book operates one level above that. It tells you which problems and concepts to write about, but not which form the resulting piece should take. Having that mapping here, from concept catalog entry to blog post, tutorial, or guide, would have closed the loop. If you have not read the first book, keep that gap in mind; if you have, you can supply the missing piece yourself.

**Verdict:** A compact, focused book that you can read in one sitting and put to use the next day. It goes deeper on strategy than <i>Developer Marketing Does Not Exist</i>, but it leans on that book for the content types and their structure, so read the two together. Recommended for anyone writing technical content for a product they want people to find.
